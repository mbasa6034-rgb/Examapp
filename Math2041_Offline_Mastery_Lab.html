01 · Real sequences — a numbered list

A sequence is an ordered list a₁,a₂,a₃,… whose terms are indexed by n. It is a function from positive integers to real numbers. It converges if its terms approach one finite number L.

limₙ→∞ aₙ = L

Convergent

Terms approach a finite limit. Example: 1/n → 0.

Divergent

Terms grow without bound or do not settle. Examples: n→∞; (−1)ⁿ oscillates.

Fast sequence-limit patterns

PatternTechniqueMemory clue
Polynomial / polynomialDivide by highest power of nSame degree → ratio of leading coefficients
ln n / nᵖLog grows slower than positive powersLimit 0 for p>0
nᵏ/eⁿExponential dominates polynomialLimit 0
√(n²+…)-nMultiply by conjugateFix ∞−∞

Worked example 1 — rational sequence

Find lim (4n²+1)/(2n²+1).
  1. Highest power is n².
  2. Divide top and bottom by n²: (4+1/n²)/(2+1/n²).
  3. As n→∞, 1/n²→0.
  4. Limit = 4/2 = 2; convergent.

Worked example 2 — conjugate

Find lim [√(n²+n)−n].
  1. Direct form is ∞−∞.
  2. Multiply numerator and denominator by √(n²+n)+n.
  3. Expression becomes n/[√(n²+n)+n].
  4. Divide by n: 1/[√(1+1/n)+1] → 1/2.

Bounded and monotone

Bounded: m≤aₙ≤M for all n; terms stay between two fences. Increasing: aₙ₊₁≥aₙ. Decreasing: aₙ₊₁≤aₙ.

Monotone Convergence Theorem: an increasing sequence bounded above converges; a decreasing sequence bounded below converges. Bounded alone does not guarantee convergence: (−1)ⁿ is bounded but oscillates.

To test monotonicity, calculate aₙ₊₁−aₙ. Positive means increasing; negative means decreasing.

02 · Infinite series — adding infinitely many terms

A series is the sum of a sequence. Its partial sum is Sₙ=a₁+…+aₙ. The infinite series converges if Sₙ approaches a finite value.

Σaₙ = limₙ→∞ Sₙ
Critical trap: aₙ→0 does NOT prove Σaₙ converges. The harmonic series Σ1/n diverges even though 1/n→0.

Geometric series

a+ar+ar²+… = Σₙ₌₀∞ arⁿ

a is the first term; r is the common ratio (divide a term by the previous term). It converges only if |r|<1, and then its sum is a/(1−r). If |r|≥1 it diverges. If indexing starts at n=1, write the first terms before using the formula.

Harmonic and p-series

Σ1/n diverges.   Σ1/nᵖ converges iff p>1; diverges for 0<p≤1.
SeriesResult
Σ1/nDiverges (harmonic)
Σ1/n²Converges
Σ1/√nDiverges, p=1/2
Σ1/n³Converges

Telescoping

Use partial fractions and cancellation. For example, 1/[n(n+1)]=1/n−1/(n+1). Then Sₙ=(1−1/2)+(1/2−1/3)+…+(1/n−1/(n+1))=1−1/(n+1)→1.

Alternating, absolute and conditional convergence

For Σ(−1)ⁿbₙ, with bₙ≥0, if bₙ decreases eventually and bₙ→0, the alternating-series test proves convergence.

  • Absolutely convergent: Σ|aₙ| converges, therefore Σaₙ converges.
  • Conditionally convergent: Σaₙ converges but Σ|aₙ| diverges.
  • Divergent: original series does not converge.
For signed series, test Σ|aₙ| first. If it converges, stop: absolute convergence. If it diverges, test the original signed series, often with the alternating test.

03 · 🔥 The method-selection engine

Do not pick a test randomly. Inspect the structure, then choose the easiest valid test.

Decision tree — memorize this order

  1. Is the question about a sequence or a series? Sequence → find lim aₙ. Series → continue.
  2. Check lim aₙ. If not zero or DNE, the series diverges immediately. If zero, continue.
  3. Clear constant ratio rⁿ? → geometric series.
  4. Looks like 1/nᵖ? → p-series, or compare with it.
  5. Polynomial fraction? → compare highest powers; LCT is often shortest.
  6. Easy inequality? → DCT.
  7. Factorial, products or cⁿ? → ratio test.
  8. Whole expression raised to n? → root test.
  9. Positive smooth decreasing logarithmic expression? → integral test may help.
  10. Alternating signs? → absolute convergence first, then alternating-series test.
  11. Power series in x? → ratio/root for radius, then test both endpoints.

🟦 DCT — Direct Comparison

Clue: an easy inequality.
0≤aₙ≤bₙ and Σbₙ converges ⇒ Σaₙ converges.
0≤bₙ≤aₙ and Σbₙ diverges ⇒ Σaₙ diverges.

Convergence: smaller than a convergent benchmark. Divergence: larger than a divergent benchmark.

🟨 LCT — Limit Comparison

Clue: same dominant powers.
L=lim(aₙ/bₙ), where 0<L<∞.

Then the positive-term series Σaₙ and Σbₙ have the same behavior. Choose bₙ from the dominant powers.

One-line memory trick: DCT = prove a size relationship using ≤ or ≥. LCT = prove same long-term size using a ratio limit. If the inequality is obvious, DCT is fine. If dominant powers make the ratio simple, LCT is usually faster.

Worked DCT — easy inequality

Test Σ1/(n²+4).
  1. Terms are positive.
  2. n²+4≥n², so 1/(n²+4)≤1/n².
  3. Σ1/n² converges because p=2>1.
  4. The smaller positive series converges. Convergent by DCT.

Worked LCT — dominant powers

Test Σ(3n²+5)/(2n⁴+7n+1).
  1. Dominant behavior: 3n²/(2n⁴)=(3/2)/n².
  2. Choose bₙ=1/n².
  3. L=lim [(3n²+5)/(2n⁴+7n+1)]/(1/n²).
  4. =lim (3n⁴+5n²)/(2n⁴+7n+1)=3/2.
  5. 0<3/2<∞ and Σ1/n² converges. Therefore original series converges by LCT.
TestRecognize it byFormula and result
RatioFactorials, products, exponential powersL=lim|aₙ₊₁/aₙ|; L<1 converge, L>1 diverge, L=1 inconclusive
RootWhole term is [f(n)]ⁿL=lim ⁿ√|aₙ|; same rules
IntegralPositive, continuous, decreasing f(n); logarithmic termsΣf(n) and ∫f(x)dx share convergence behavior

Ratio example — factorial

For aₙ=n!/2ⁿ², aₙ₊₁/aₙ=(n+1)/2⁽²ⁿ⁺¹⁾→0<1. The series converges by ratio test. Factorials simplify when comparing consecutive terms.

Integral example — logarithm

Σₙ₌₂∞1/(n ln n): ratio test gives 1 (inconclusive). Let f(x)=1/(x ln x), positive, continuous and decreasing for x≥2. ∫₂∞dx/(x ln x)=lim ln(ln x)=∞, so the series diverges.
Never interpret L=1 as divergence. It means the ratio/root test gives no conclusion. Change tests.

04 · All tests — exam-ready steps

1. nth-term divergence testFind lim aₙ. If it is nonzero, infinite, or DNE, Σaₙ diverges. If it is zero, the test is inconclusive. Exam sentence: “Since lim aₙ≠0, the series diverges by the nth-term test.”
2. Direct comparison (DCT)Confirm positive terms → choose known benchmark → write inequality → state benchmark behavior → use correct direction. For convergence, smaller than convergent. For divergence, larger than divergent.
3. Limit comparison (LCT)Find dominant powers → choose bₙ, often 1/nᵖ → calculate lim aₙ/bₙ → if finite and positive, same behavior. If L=0 or ∞, do not claim equivalence; use a justified one-sided comparison instead.
4. Ratio testWrite aₙ₊₁, form |aₙ₊₁/aₙ|, cancel factorials and powers, take limit. L<1 absolutely convergent; L>1 divergent; L=1 inconclusive.
5. Root testTake nth root of the absolute value of the whole term. Simplify ⁿ√(cⁿ)=c. Apply same L<1, L>1, L=1 rules.
6. Integral testDefine f(x)=aₓ; verify positive, continuous and decreasing eventually; evaluate improper integral as a limit; transfer result to the series.
7. Alternating-series testFor Σ(−1)ⁿbₙ check bₙ≥0, bₙ decreases eventually and bₙ→0. Then the original alternating series converges. Test Σbₙ separately for absolute or conditional.
8. Geometric and telescopingGeometric: find first term and common ratio; |r|<1. Telescoping: write partial sums and show cancellation, keeping first and last surviving terms.

05 · Power series — radius and interval

Σₙ₌₀∞ cₙ(x−a)ⁿ

a is center; x is variable. The radius R tells the distance from the center where convergence is guaranteed.

  1. Identify center a from (x−a)ⁿ.
  2. Apply ratio/root test to general term with x fixed.
  3. Solve condition L<1 to get |x−a|<R.
  4. Write open interval (a−R,a+R).
  5. Substitute each endpoint into the original series separately.
  6. Include only endpoints where the series converges.

Worked example — geometric power series

Σ(3/4)ⁿ(x+5)ⁿ = Σ[(3/4)(x+5)]ⁿ. Ratio r=(3/4)(x+5). Require |r|<1, so |x+5|<4/3. Center −5, R=4/3, open interval (−19/3,−11/3). At either endpoint the geometric ratio is ±1, so terms do not approach zero. Final interval: (−19/3,−11/3).

Worked example — one endpoint included

Σ3ⁿ(x+4)ⁿ/√n. Root test gives |3(x+4)|<1, so center −4, R=1/3, open interval (−13/3,−11/3). Left endpoint gives alternating Σ(−1)ⁿ/√n, convergent by alternating test. Right endpoint gives Σ1/√n, divergent p-series. Final interval: [−13/3,−11/3).
Most common mistake: finding R and stopping. Radius is not the interval. Always test both endpoints in the original series.

Differentiate/integrate a power series

d/dx Σcₙ(x−a)ⁿ = Σn cₙ(x−a)ⁿ⁻¹
∫Σcₙ(x−a)ⁿdx = C+Σ[cₙ/(n+1)](x−a)ⁿ⁺¹

Within the interval of convergence, term-by-term differentiation/integration is valid; radius stays the same, but recheck endpoints.

Use cosh u=1+u²/2!+u⁴/4!+u⁶/6!+… and substitute u=3x:

  1. Rewrite as 2x³/[1+x/2].
  2. Use 1/(1+u)=1−u+u²−u³+… for |u|<1.
  3. Set u=x/2 and multiply by 2x³.
2x³−x⁴+x⁵/2−x⁶/4+… , |x|<2

Derive the arctan identity

  2. Integrate from 0 to x.
  3. Left side integrates to arctan x; integrate each power on the right.

09 · 🎯 Fresh six-question mock exam

Separate from HT1 and worksheet. Try each question on paper before opening its solution.

Mock 1 · Sequence limit
aₙ=(5n²−3n+1)/(2n²+7)
5/2, convergent. Divide numerator and denominator by n²: (5−3/n+1/n²)/(2+7/n²)→5/2. Clue: rational sequence, same highest degree.
Mock 2 · DCT or LCT?
Σₙ₌₁∞1/(n²+3n)
Converges by DCT. n²+3n≥n², so 0<1/(n²+3n)≤1/n². Benchmark Σ1/n² converges (p=2>1). Easy inequality is the DCT clue.
Mock 3 · LCT
Σₙ₌₁∞(4n+1)/(n²+5n+2)
Diverges by LCT. Dominant behavior is 4/n. Choose bₙ=1/n. Ratio limit = lim(4n²+n)/(n²+5n+2)=4. Since 0<4<∞, same behavior as harmonic series Σ1/n, which diverges.
Mock 4 · Ratio test
Σₙ₌₁∞5ⁿ/n!
Converges. aₙ₊₁/aₙ=[5ⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)!]·[n!/5ⁿ]=5/(n+1)→0<1. Factorial simplifies across consecutive terms: ratio test.
Mock 5 · Integral test
Σₙ₌₂∞1/[n(ln n)²]
Converges. f(x)=1/[x(ln x)²] is positive, continuous and decreasing for x≥2. Substitute u=ln x, du=dx/x: ∫₂∞dx/[x(ln x)²]=∫ln2∞u⁻²du=1/ln2, finite. Therefore the series converges.
Mock 6 · Radius and interval
Σₙ₌₁∞(x−2)ⁿ/n
R=1; interval [1,3). Ratio limit is |x−2|, so |x−2|<1. At x=1: alternating harmonic converges. At x=3: harmonic diverges. Include left endpoint only.

Exam answer structure

  1. Name the method: “I use LCT with bₙ=1/n².”
  2. Show the calculation: ratio limit or inequality.
  3. State the benchmark: p-series p=2 converges.
  4. Connect: 0<L<∞ means same behavior.
  5. Conclude: “Therefore, the given series converges.”
